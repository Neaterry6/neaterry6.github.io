Tirana has fought five days straight, and Albania's students say they are done waiting

BBC News · 2026-10-18 · 0 views

Demonstrators and riot police fought pitched battles in the centre of Tirana on Saturday for the fifth day in a row. Masked protesters, many of them high school and university students, hurled rocks and petrol bombs at riot police defending the education ministry, one of the focal points of the protests. Police answered with water cannon and volleys of tear gas, and there are reports that rubber bullets were used for the first time.

Many of the protesters wore gas masks while volunteers handed out syringes of saline eyewash to reduce the pain from the tear gas billowing down the street. The constant boom of petrol bombs could be heard exploding. During the afternoon the focus shifted to Prime Minister Edi Rama's office on the city's main boulevard, where the crowds swelled far beyond previous days. Riot police faced a steady hail of cobblestones and fireworks and replied with barrage after barrage of tear gas, some canisters landing far back in the crowds and causing panic.

Black smoke from a burning vehicle and the sirens of police cars and ambulances split the afternoon. As dusk fell, police special forces arrived to reinforce the riot police. Many people have been injured on both sides. Eighty have been charged with disorder so far, and hundreds more have been arrested or are under investigation. The centre of Tirana looks increasingly like a war zone, scattered with broken glass and burnt debris.

What they are asking for

Protests began in early June and have grown into a wider anti-government movement calling for Rama to resign over corruption allegations linked to his administration. The students say they are against "the whole corrupt political class".

George, a 19-year-old medical student who works as an electrician to fund his studies, stood near the burnt-out education ministry with a scarf round his face and goggles over his eyes, clutching two rocks intended for the police. Asked if he was concerned about injuring them, he said: "We have mothers too. We have family too. We were peaceful for 135 days. Now we are trying to do like in France… we are trying to make a revolution. Our government doesn't want peace, so we are using violence."

Klea, 17, is in her first year at university in Tirana. She said: "All the students are boycotting their university classes and left school to fight for our rights." She alleged there were provocateurs among the protesters smashing up stores and banks to discredit the demonstrations.

Osman Stafa, a prominent journalist for the News24 channel who also describes himself as an activist for change, called for a "neutral, caretaker government for one year, to organise new elections".

Why it matters

  • Rama has been in power 13 years and dominates Albanian politics, much like his main rival Sali Berisha of the opposition Democratic Party did for decades. His Socialist Party holds 83 of 140 seats and the next parliamentary elections are not due until 2029. There is no electoral route out of this, which is exactly why the street is the venue.
  • The corruption pressure is not only from the street. Prosecutors set up under EU and US pressure in 2019 recently arrested Vlora Hyseni, the powerful head of the secret service, accused of helping businessman Ergys Agasi — a former government official and personal friend of Rama — evade justice. Agasi denies the allegations through his lawyer; Hyseni told prosecutors she contacted him to try to recruit him for the intelligence services. Rama admits knowing Agasi but denies knowledge of illegal transactions.
  • Rama blamed the opposition Democratic Party and what he called "the sinister agenda of foreign actors, intent on halting our progress" towards EU membership, which he has promised by 2030. He did not name them. He also urged Albanians to "not forget that we have achieved extraordinary things".
  • That is an awkward line for Brussels, because Rama has long been viewed favourably by the EU, which Albania aspires to join. An ally whose streets are on fire over corruption is a harder story to sell than one whose courts are quietly working.
  • As analyst Lutfi Dervishi put it: "He's the victim of his own success. By concentrating so much power in his own hands, he has made himself the only person the people blame for their grievances."
