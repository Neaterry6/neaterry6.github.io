An Irish priest killed at Passchendaele while going to help wounded men is a step closer to sainthood

BBC News · 2026-10-18 · 0 views

Fr Willie Doyle, an Irish Jesuit who served as a British Army chaplain through some of the worst fighting of the First World War, moves a step towards possible canonisation on Sunday, when the Catholic Church in Ireland holds a service in Mullingar Cathedral before sending thousands of pages of evidence to the Vatican.

Born William Joseph Gabriel Doyle in Dalkey in 1873, he had a comfortable middle-class childhood, was sent to a Catholic boarding school in England, and returned to Ireland as a teenager. By 18 he had begun studying to be a Jesuit priest. World War One had been raging for over a year when he volunteered as a military chaplain to the 16th (Irish) Division.

He served with the division for almost two years, much of it in the trenches on the Western Front, until he was killed by shellfire at Passchendaele on 16 August 1917. He was in his early 40s. He died going to the aid of two wounded Protestant soldiers, one of them a young officer from Belfast, 2nd Lt Arthur Vivian Green.

Why the Church calls him a martyr of charity

Because of that, the Catholic Church in Ireland considers Fr Doyle an "ecumenical martyr of charity" — someone who died ministering to soldiers of other Christian denominations. He was awarded the Military Cross for bravery at the Somme and mentioned in despatches several times. Maj-Gen Sir William Bernard Hickie, who commanded the 16th Division, called him "the bravest man that he ever met".

Why it matters

  • The case rests on a paper trail as much as on courage. Fr Doyle was a prolific letter writer, and Patrick Kenny — the business school lecturer who founded the Fr Willie Doyle Association in 2020 and lives near where Doyle grew up — argues that his correspondence has become an important eyewitness source for historians. "If you look at any book about the Irish in the First World War, almost invariably Fr Willie Doyle is in that book."
  • The diocesan inquiry opened in 2022 and produced close to 3,500 pages: a theological commission studying his writings, a historical commission studying his life, and a tribunal that interviewed people about the religious legacy he inspired. That documentation is what now goes to Rome.
  • It is early days. Canonisation has five stages and this one is at the first, so the realistic expectation is a process measured in years or decades, not an announcement.
  • Bishop of Meath Tom Deenihan argues the example of an Irish Catholic priest who died serving as a British Army chaplain "has much to offer our own time when nationality and denomination run the risk of polarity", and says Fr Doyle's spirituality "resonates very much with that of the spirituality of Gen Z". He added he is "confident" the case will succeed.
  • There is a harder detail in the record that the campaign does not lead with: Kenny notes Fr Doyle was known for kindness and mercy towards wounded German prisoners-of-war, which in 1917 was not a given.

The service in Mullingar on Sunday afternoon marks the final public session of the tribunal of inquiry. In line with tradition, the parcels of evidence have been wrapped in red ribbon and sealed with wax by the bishop; the remaining documents, including the minutes of the final session, will be sealed at the ceremony.
