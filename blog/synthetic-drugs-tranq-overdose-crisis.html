US overdose deaths are falling, and the drugs killing people are getting harder to reverse

BBC News · 2026-10-18 · 0 views

Overdose deaths in the US have fallen for three years running — from a peak of 110,000 in 2022 to around 70,000 last year — and the BBC's report from Columbus, Ohio, and Chicago explains why that number is a misleading comfort.

The drug supply has changed. Fentanyl, entirely man-made and 50 to 100 times more powerful than heroin, is now routinely cut with synthetic animal tranquillisers: xylazine and medetomidine, both known on the street as "tranq". They prolong the high. They also slow breathing, and naloxone — the nasal spray that reverses opioid overdoses and has saved tens of thousands of lives — does not work against them.

The BBC's team carried naloxone in their pockets throughout. Their guide, Mike, a 48-year-old army veteran with a 20-year opioid addiction, was almost certainly beyond its help. His pupils shrank to pin pricks as he smoked three or four dollars' worth of fentanyl from silver foil in an alleyway. Asked what his drugs were cut with, he said: "Xylazine, and the one we can't pronounce." A drug sample he handed in later tested positive for medetomidine.

The crisis moved, it did not shrink

  • "In the last 12 months in Franklin County [Columbus], 78% of fatal overdoses are from stimulants," said Scott Sanders, who runs the recovery group Hope Dealers and works with the CDC on a drug-sampling programme.
  • The mechanism is a "hot dose": people buying crack cocaine are getting crack spiked with fentanyl or another opioid cocktail, and overdosing without knowing it. Sanders said overdose deaths among Black men in Ohio are actually rising.
  • Xylazine also causes necrotic skin ulcers, wounds deep enough to reach bone and difficult to treat.
  • At a Hope Dealer meeting at Glenwood Church, most of the room raised a hand when asked who had overdosed. One man described being hit with naloxone three times by paramedics in 2022 before a fourth attempt saved him.

Why it matters

  • The headline number is down and the danger is up. A falling death toll with a supply this unpredictable means the people still using are further from help, not safer.
  • Naloxone's success is the reason the crisis is being misread. The drug that drove the decline does not touch the sedatives now in the mix, so the same reversal that saved thousands is silently failing on a growing share of overdoses.
  • "The tragedy is stimulant users who are purchasing crack think that's what they're getting, and they're not," Sanders said. That is a different problem from opioid addiction, and it catches people who never considered themselves at risk.
  • The human cost runs through families. Karen, 17 years clean, was on her way to attempt suicide the day she found out she was pregnant; she got clean in Cook County jail and her daughter Abby was born inside it. Abby, now 17, says she has seen drugs "killing my aunt, my uncles" and it is "not worth it to get high for like a couple of hours".
  • Enforcement alone has not worked through several administrations, and the dealers adapt faster. The sheriff the BBC accompanied argued treatment has to be "front and centre" alongside supply-side policing.
