France's student protests turned into a night of arson and looting, and the arrest toll passed 1,000

BBC News · 2026-10-18 · 0 views

Violence broke out across France on Saturday during the latest day of demonstrations against education standards and the cost of living. An estimated 550,000 people took to the streets. The interior ministry, which described an "escalation in violence", put arrests at at least 400 in its initial count; the BBC's own headline later in the evening put the figure above 1,000 as disorder spread.

What was burning

In Paris there were significant clashes into the evening, with riot police using tear gas and water cannon. Fires were lit at the Place de la République and vehicles torched. La Barrique, a bar in the capital, was set alight, shops were ransacked and at least one restaurant was gutted.

Elsewhere the damage was worse. In Limoges a large fire at the town hall caused part of the building to collapse; the local prefecture said it was "largely lost". In Lyon, demonstrators burned the city's tourist office. Mayor Grégory Doucet called it "a tragedy" for the city: "it breaks our hearts because it's a very iconic, well-known historic building." Police said "hostile individuals" stopped firefighters tackling the blaze safely.

Looting hit several cities. Clothes and food shops were raided in Paris, and an Apple store in Lille was broken into and emptied. Damage to buildings was reported in Grenoble, Avignon, Pau, Montpellier and Valence. BBC Verify authenticated footage of a tourist train in Grenoble being driven by masked men along a narrow street towards at least three riot police officers.

Sixty law enforcement officers were injured, two of them taken to hospital unconscious, according to interior minister Laurent Nuñez. In Saint-Étienne, mayor Régis Juanico said a young man "had his hand blown off by a grenade".

Why it matters

  • The trigger is unglamorous and specific: hundreds of thousands of students have been protesting for weeks over staff shortages and dilapidated buildings in lycées, the secondary schools attended by 15 to 18-year-olds. The education minister promised 3,000 extra supply teachers on 8 October. The protests continued and got bigger.
  • Saturday's crowds were swollen by trade unions and the gilets jaunes movement, which ran for over a year from 2018. That is how a schools dispute becomes a cost-of-living revolt.
  • The political frame is already being fought over. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said police faced "violence of extreme intensity" and that some groups were using the protests as a "warm-up" for the presidential election: "No-one will impose their political agenda through violence." He called Mélenchon's accusation that police "unleashed" on protesters "dangerous and cynical". Marine Le Pen said legitimate demands had been "held hostage by the far left".
  • Macron said those who "looted even our town halls, will be prosecuted and punished". With him leaving office next year and April's election shaping up as Le Pen versus Mélenchon, every weekend of disorder is now campaign material.
